# Chaos theory — three principles

Williams frames "chaos" not as randomness but, in his words, as a much higher form of order — really a name for how a system handles new information. He grounds this in a potted history-of-science arc (Aristotle's orderly logic vs. Heraclitus's "you can't step in the same river twice," then relativity, quantum mechanics, Bell's theorem on non-locality, and David Bohm's stronger claim of universal interconnectedness) before stating three operating principles:[^practical-fractal-video]

1. **Everything takes the path of least resistance.**
2. **That path is shaped by an underlying structure that is usually unseen** (a riverbed shapes the rapids; a building's layout unconsciously shapes how you walk through it).
3. **That structure can be discovered — and changed.** He compares this to a fear response learned in early childhood (e.g. "don't touch the stove"): learned before we could reason about it, and therefore re-learnable.

## The causal chain this implies for markets

Applying the three principles to price action, Williams orders market causation as:[^practical-fractal-video]

1. Change in trader attitude/sentiment (dispersed, unorganized)
2. Change in volume (positions opening/closing)
3. Change in momentum (direction)
4. Change in the *speed* of momentum — acceleration/deceleration
5. Change in price — **always the effect, never the cause**

This ordering is the stated justification for trading the [five dimensions of market structure](/concepts/five-dimensions-of-market-structure.md) instead of price alone: momentum and acceleration sit further up the causal chain, so they change first.

## Once something is organized, it defends itself

A secondary theme: once new information gets forced into an existing mental category (he gives a personal anecdote about an unfair snap judgment formed from one bad experience), that "organized" belief — like a bureaucracy — starts prioritizing its own survival over accuracy. He uses this to explain why established trends "want" to continue, citing 1980s skepticism that the Dow could pass 3,000, disproven by its later rise past 3,700.[^practical-fractal-video]
